Kinematics

Kinematics describes how things move, without asking why. With three quantities (position, velocity and acceleration) and a handful of equations, you can predict where a car, a ball or a planet will be at any moment.

Position and displacement

Position (x) says where an object is, measured from a chosen origin along a chosen axis. Positions to one side of the origin are positive; to the other side, negative.

Displacement (Δx) is the change in position: where you ended minus where you started. It has a direction, so it can be negative.

Distance is the total length of the path travelled. It is never negative. Walk 3 m east and then 3 m back west: your distance is 6 m, your displacement is zero.

Speed and velocity

Velocity is the rate at which position changes:

v = Δx / Δt metres per second (m/s)

Velocity is a vector: its sign (or, in two dimensions, its direction) tells you which way the object is going. Speed is the size of the velocity and ignores direction. A car going around a roundabout at a steady 30 km/h has constant speed but a constantly changing velocity.

An average velocity covers a whole time interval. An instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one moment, which is what a speedometer shows.

Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes:

a = Δv / Δt metres per second, per second (m/s²)

An acceleration of 3 m/s² means the velocity grows by 3 m/s each second: 0, 3, 6, 9 m/s…

The sign of the acceleration alone does not say whether an object speeds up or slows down. Compare it with the velocity:

  • Velocity and acceleration in the same direction: the object speeds up.
  • Velocity and acceleration in opposite directions: the object slows down.

A car moving in the negative direction with negative acceleration is getting faster, not slower.

Try it. In the Straight line scene, set the starting velocity to 15 m/s and the acceleration to −3 m/s², then press Start. The car slows, stops for an instant, and comes back. The acceleration never changed.

Reading motion graphs

Two graphs tell the whole story of a motion along a line.

Position against time

  • The slope is the velocity. A steep line means fast; a flat line means at rest.
  • A straight line means constant velocity. A curve means the velocity is changing.

Velocity against time

  • The slope is the acceleration.
  • The area between the line and the time axis is the displacement. Area below the axis counts as negative.

Try it. Run the car with zero acceleration, then with a positive one. With zero acceleration the position graph is a straight line. With constant acceleration it becomes a parabola, while the velocity graph is a straight sloping line. The shaded area under the velocity graph always equals the car's displacement.

The constant-acceleration equations

When the acceleration is constant, four equations connect displacement, time, starting velocity v0, final velocity v and acceleration a.

v = v0 + at
Δx = v0t + ½at²
v² = v0² + 2aΔx
Δx = ½(v0 + v)t

Each equation leaves out one quantity. To solve a problem, list what you know and what you want, then pick the equation that contains exactly those.

Worked example

A car travelling at 20 m/s brakes with a constant deceleration of 5 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping?

Known: v0 = 20 m/s, v = 0, a = −5 m/s². Time is neither given nor wanted, so use the third equation:

0 = 20² + 2(−5)Δx, which gives Δx = 400 / 10 = 40 m.

Notice the square. At double the speed (40 m/s) the braking distance is four times as long: 160 m.

Free fall

Near the Earth's surface, and ignoring air resistance, every falling object has the same downward acceleration:

g ≈ 9.8 m/s²

This is true whatever the object's mass, and whether it is moving up, moving down or momentarily at rest at the top of its flight. A ball thrown straight up slows by 9.8 m/s each second, stops for an instant, then gains 9.8 m/s each second on the way down. Its velocity is zero at the top; its acceleration is not.

Free fall is simply constant-acceleration motion along a vertical line, so all four equations apply with a = −g (taking up as positive).

Worked example

A stone is dropped from a bridge and hits the water 2.0 s later. How high is the bridge?

Δy = v0t + ½at² = 0 + ½(−9.8)(2.0)² = −19.6 m. The bridge is about 20 m above the water.

Projectile motion

A projectile is any object that moves under gravity alone after being launched: a kicked ball, a jet of water, a long jumper. Its path looks complicated, but one idea makes it simple:

The horizontal and vertical parts of the motion are independent. Each can be analysed separately; they share only the time.

  • Horizontally there is no force (ignoring air), so the horizontal velocity never changes.
  • Vertically the motion is free fall with acceleration g downward.

For a launch at speed v0 and angle θ above the horizontal, the starting velocity splits into components:

vx = v0 cos θ    vy = v0 sin θ

Then, at any time t:

x = vxt    y = vyt − ½gt²

Combining a steady sideways drift with an accelerating fall produces a parabola.

A striking consequence: a bullet fired horizontally and a bullet dropped from the same height at the same instant hit level ground at the same time. The sideways motion has no effect on the fall.

Range, height and flight time

For a launch from level ground:

R = v0² sin 2θ / g range: horizontal distance to landing
H = (v0 sin θ)² / 2g maximum height
T = 2v0 sin θ / g time of flight

Because sin 2θ is largest when 2θ = 90°, the range is greatest at a launch angle of 45°. Two angles that add up to 90°, such as 30° and 60°, give the same range: one path is low and fast, the other high and slow.

Try it. In the Projectile scene, launch at 30°, then at 60°, without clearing. Both shots land at the same mark. Watch the two velocity arrows on the ball: the horizontal one never changes; the vertical one shrinks, reverses and grows. Then switch gravity to the Moon.

Common mistakes

  • "Zero velocity means zero acceleration." At the top of a throw the velocity is zero but the acceleration is still 9.8 m/s² downward.
  • "Negative acceleration means slowing down." Only if the velocity is positive. Compare the two signs.
  • Mixing up distance and displacement, or speed and velocity. Check whether direction matters in the question.
  • Using these equations when acceleration is not constant. They are valid only for constant acceleration.
  • Mixing horizontal and vertical quantities in one equation for a projectile. Keep two separate lists.

Check yourself

A runner completes one lap of a 400 m track in 80 s. What are the average speed and the average velocity?

Average speed = distance / time = 400 / 80 = 5 m/s. The runner ends where they started, so the displacement is zero and the average velocity is zero.

A train starts from rest and accelerates at 0.5 m/s² for 40 s. How fast is it going, and how far has it travelled?

v = v0 + at = 0 + 0.5 × 40 = 20 m/s. Δx = ½at² = ½ × 0.5 × 1600 = 400 m.

A ball is thrown straight up at 14.7 m/s. How long until it returns to your hand?

It loses 9.8 m/s every second, so it reaches the top after 14.7 / 9.8 = 1.5 s. The way down takes the same time, so the total is 3.0 s.

A ball rolls off a 1.25 m high table at 3 m/s. How far from the table does it land? (Use g = 10 m/s².)

Vertical: 1.25 = ½ × 10 × t², so t = 0.5 s. Horizontal: x = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5 m. The fall time does not depend on the horizontal speed.

Key points

  • Velocity is the rate of change of position; acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.
  • On a position–time graph, slope is velocity. On a velocity–time graph, slope is acceleration and area is displacement.
  • Four equations cover all motion with constant acceleration.
  • Free fall is constant acceleration at g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward, for all masses.
  • A projectile combines constant horizontal velocity with vertical free fall.
