Newton's Laws of Motion

Three short statements, published by Isaac Newton in 1687, explain why anything speeds up, slows down or changes direction. Almost all of mechanics is built on them.

Force and net force

A force is a push or a pull. It has a size and a direction, so it is a vector. Force is measured in newtons (N); one newton is roughly the weight of a small apple.

Objects usually feel several forces at once. What matters for motion is their vector sum, the net force. Two people pushing a crate with 50 N each in the same direction give a net force of 100 N. Pushing in opposite directions, they give a net force of zero, and the crate behaves as if nobody were pushing at all.

When the net force is zero, the forces are balanced. When it is not zero, they are unbalanced. Every one of Newton's laws is a statement about net force.

The first law: inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving at constant speed in a straight line, unless a net force acts on it.

The first half feels obvious: a book on a table does not start sliding by itself. The second half is the surprising part. It says that motion does not need a cause. Only a change in motion needs a cause.

This contradicts everyday experience. A ball rolled across the floor stops. A car with the engine off coasts to a halt. For two thousand years people concluded, with Aristotle, that the natural state of things is rest and that something must keep pushing an object to keep it moving.

Galileo saw the flaw. The ball stops because the floor and the air push back on it. Make the floor smoother and the ball rolls farther. Imagine a perfectly smooth floor and no air, and there is nothing left to stop it: it would roll forever. Friction is a force like any other. Things on Earth stop because a force acts, exactly as the law says.

The tendency of an object to keep doing what it is already doing is called inertia. Mass is the measure of inertia: the more mass an object has, the harder it is to start, stop or turn.

Try it. In the lab, choose No friction and give the block one short push. Watch the speed and the net force. With the net force at zero, the speed never changes. Then switch to High friction and push again.

Where you feel inertia

  • A bus brakes and you lurch forward. Nothing pushed you; your body simply kept moving while the bus slowed.
  • A seat belt supplies the force that changes your motion in a crash. Without it, you continue at the car's original speed.
  • A spacecraft far from any planet coasts for years with its engines off.

The second law: force, mass and acceleration

The first law says what happens when the net force is zero. The second law says what happens when it is not.

The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force on it and inversely proportional to its mass. It points in the direction of the net force.

Fnet = ma net force (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m/s²)

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. An acceleration of 2 m/s² means the velocity changes by 2 m/s every second. Slowing down is also acceleration; it just points against the motion.

The equation contains two separate ideas:

  • More force, more acceleration. Double the net force on the same object and its acceleration doubles.
  • More mass, less acceleration. Apply the same force to twice the mass and the acceleration is halved. That is why an empty shopping trolley is easy to get moving and a full one is not.

The law also defines the newton: 1 N is the net force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s².

Worked example

A 4 kg block is pushed with 10 N across a floor that exerts 2 N of friction. What is its acceleration?

The forces point in opposite directions, so the net force is 10 − 2 = 8 N. Then a = Fnet / m = 8 / 4 = 2 m/s², in the direction of the push.

After 3 seconds, starting from rest, the block moves at 2 × 3 = 6 m/s.

Try it. Set the force to 4 N with no friction. Hold Push right with the 1 kg block and note the acceleration. Reset, choose 2 kg, then 4 kg. The force is the same each time; the acceleration is halved, then halved again. The speed graph gets less steep, because its slope is the acceleration.

Mass is not weight

Mass is the amount of inertia an object has, measured in kilograms. It is the same everywhere. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass, measured in newtons:

W = mg g ≈ 9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface

A 70 kg person weighs about 686 N on Earth and about 113 N on the Moon, yet their mass is 70 kg in both places. They would be just as hard to push sideways on the Moon as on Earth.

Friction, in brief

Friction acts along a surface and opposes sliding. A block at rest stays at rest until the push exceeds the maximum friction the surface can supply; below that, friction simply matches the push and the net force stays zero. Once the block slides, friction has a roughly constant size that depends on the surfaces and on how hard they are pressed together.

Try it. Choose High friction and the 4 kg block, then lower the force to 4 N and push. Nothing moves: friction balances your push. Raise the force until the block breaks free.

The third law: action and reaction

When object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction.

Forces always come in pairs. You cannot touch without being touched. Push on a wall with 100 N and the wall pushes on you with 100 N. The two forces of a pair:

  • are equal in size and opposite in direction,
  • are the same kind of force (both contact, both gravity, and so on),
  • act on two different objects.

The last point resolves the classic puzzle: if the forces are equal and opposite, why don't they cancel? Forces cancel only when they act on the same object. The force on A affects A's motion; the force on B affects B's motion.

Equal forces do not mean equal effects. By the second law, the same force gives a small mass a large acceleration and a large mass a small one. When you jump, you push the Earth down exactly as hard as it pushes you up. You accelerate noticeably. The Earth, with a mass about 1023 times larger, does not.

Try it. Open the Action and reaction scene. Give the two skaters different masses and press Push off. The force arrows are always the same length. The lighter skater leaves faster.

Third-law pairs around you

  • Walking: your foot pushes the ground backward; the ground pushes you forward.
  • Rockets: the engine pushes exhaust gas backward; the gas pushes the rocket forward. No air is needed to push against.
  • Swimming: you push water back; the water pushes you ahead.
  • A falling apple: the Earth pulls the apple down; the apple pulls the Earth up with the same force.

Common mistakes

  • "A moving object must have a force on it." No. A net force is needed only to change the velocity.
  • "Heavier objects fall faster." Without air resistance, all objects fall with the same acceleration. A heavier object feels more gravity but has proportionally more inertia.
  • "The stronger object exerts the bigger force." In any interaction the two forces are equal, whether it is a lorry hitting a fly or the Earth pulling the Moon.
  • "Zero net force means no motion." It means no change in motion. Constant velocity is as much a zero-net-force state as rest.

Check yourself

A hockey puck slides across ice at constant velocity. What is the net force on it?

Zero. Constant velocity means no acceleration, and by the second law no acceleration means no net force. (Real ice has a little friction, so a real puck slowly loses speed.)

A 1200 kg car accelerates at 2.5 m/s². What net force acts on it?

Fnet = ma = 1200 × 2.5 = 3000 N, in the direction of the acceleration.

A horse pulls a cart forward. The cart pulls back on the horse equally hard. How can they ever start moving?

Those two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel. Look only at the cart: the horse's pull acts forward on it, and if that pull is larger than the friction on the cart, the cart accelerates. Look only at the horse: the ground pushes it forward (the reaction to its hooves pushing back) harder than the cart pulls it back.

You stand on a bathroom scale in a lift that is accelerating upward. Does the scale read more or less than usual?

More. To accelerate you upward, the net force on you must point up, so the scale must push up with more than your weight. The scale shows the force it exerts, so the reading rises.

Key points

  • Net force is the vector sum of all forces on one object.
  • First law: zero net force means constant velocity (which includes rest).
  • Second law: Fnet = ma.
  • Third law: forces come in equal, opposite pairs acting on different objects.
  • Mass measures inertia (kg); weight is the force of gravity (N).
